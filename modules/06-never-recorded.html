Module 6  ·  The part that was never recorded

A seismic survey does not record the low frequencies

A survey holds a band of frequencies — roughly 8 Hz to 60 Hz on a good marine line — and nothing below it. Impedance in the ground rises steadily with depth as rocks compact, and that rise is far slower than 8 Hz. So is the overall level, which is zero frequency. Neither was ever recorded, and no processing can put them back. They have to come from a well. This module measures how much of an impedance answer that turns out to be, on a real log.

Where you are looking

What the survey recorded

Four short steps, on a well drilled in 1970 that is still open file. The first three work at the well; the fourth spreads the argument across a line.

1Split the log at the bottom of the seismic band

One part a survey can record, one it cannot

This is a real acoustic impedance log from Maui-4, a well drilled off Taranaki in 1970. The left panel is the whole log.

Split it at the lowest frequency the survey holds. Everything below that cut is the low-frequency component of the impedance: the overall level, and the steady increase with depth as the rocks compact. Everything above it is the seismic-band component, which is the wiggle around that trend — the beds, the coal, the sands.

A survey records none of the low-frequency component. Seismic sources put very little energy below about 6 to 8 Hz, geophones are not sensitive there, and processing removes what does arrive along with the ground roll. Zero frequency, which is the level itself, is not recorded by any survey at all.

The seismic-band component is not fully recorded either. Module 3 showed the wavelet merging anything thinner than about a quarter of its wavelength, so the finest detail on this log is lost as well. What a survey holds is a band in the middle: above the compaction trend, below the thin beds.

The sizes are the point of this step. In the window this page opens on, the low-frequency component is around nine thousand impedance units and the seismic-band component wiggles by around one and a half thousand. The readouts give both, and the last one gives the share of the final number that the survey never held. Move the window and all three numbers move with it, between about 86 and 91 percent on this well.

Common misconception

"Better processing could recover the low frequencies."

Drag the lowest-recorded-frequency control from 25 Hz down to 2 Hz, which is far better than any real survey. The share barely moves. It sits near 86 percent the whole way. The missing part is not hiding in the data waiting to be brought out. It was never recorded, and filling it in means getting it from somewhere else.

2Filling it in from a well

The two parts added back together

This step puts the two pieces back together, which is what an inversion does. The trace is summed, the way module 1 had you do it, and that gives a curve with the right shape and no level. The curve is relative acoustic impedance. It uses the recording alone, with no well logs.

The missing low-frequency component is then supplied. It is estimated by taking the impedance logs from whatever wells are available, filtering them down to the band the seismic does not cover, and interpolating that filtered version across the survey along mapped horizons. The estimate is the low-frequency model, also called the background model, and adding it to the relative curve turns a shape into impedance values.

Two names that sound alike are worth keeping apart. The low-frequency component is a property of the earth, and it exists whether or not anyone measures it. The low-frequency model is an estimate of that component, built by a person from wells and horizons. Everything the model gets wrong is carried straight into the answer, and nothing in the seismic will reveal that it is wrong.

The panels show the two pieces and the sum. The sum tracks the real log closely, which is the encouraging part. The readout underneath is the uncomfortable part. It gives how much of the final number came from the well rather than from the seismic.

Why this matters

An impedance section looks like a measurement of the earth, and most of every number on it was carried in from a well. That does not make it wrong. It does mean the reliability of the numbers depends on how good the wells were and how far away they are, which is a question about the project rather than about the seismic.

3What if the well is somewhere else

Move the background further away and watch

Wells are not where you need them. The background at your target is built from wells some distance off, and the rock changes in between.

The a well this far away control swaps the background for one built from a different part of the section, standing in for a well that has drilled slightly different rock.

Watch the two readouts separately. Shape agreement sits at about 0.93 and stays there, with no trend at all, because the shape came from the seismic and the seismic has not changed. The impedance error climbs, from about 915 units at this spot to about 1,030 at the furthest well, because the level came from the well and the well has changed.

That is a rise of roughly 12 percent, a mild version of the effect. These stand-in wells differ from each other by only a few percent, so the shift is small. A genuinely different well in a different part of a basin can be much further out, and the shape would still not notice.

Move the window and the climb stops being tidy. At some settings a more distant interval happens to fit the level better than the nearby one, and the error dips before it rises. That is real rather than a fault in the exercise: distance from a well makes a background less reliable, it does not make it reliably worse. What holds at every setting is the split between the two readouts. The shape does not respond to the well at all, and the level responds to nothing else.

That split is the practical rule to take away. Comparing one part of a section with another is sound, because both parts got the same background. Quoting a number and comparing it with a laboratory measurement or a rock-physics relationship is a different matter, and it depends on a background you did not measure.

Common misconception

"If the inversion matches the seismic well, the impedances must be right."

Every background on this page produces an answer that fits the seismic equally well, because none of them changed the seismic part at all. The fit cannot see the background, so it cannot warn you when the background is wrong. A good match to the data is not evidence that the numbers are right. Only a well can check that, and the well is usually the thing that supplied them.

4The same argument, on a seismic line

Move the well along the line and watch which panels change

Steps 1 to 3 worked on one trace at a well, because that is where the arithmetic is visible. A project is a line or a volume, and the same argument looks different when it is spread out sideways. This step is a synthetic 2D line over a low-relief anticline. A wedge sand thins across it, and a sand at 412 ms holds gas over the crest and brine off the flank.

The four panels are the four things that exist in a project. The top left is the earth: the true impedance, which nobody has. The top right is what the survey recorded, which is that impedance differenced into reflection coefficients and convolved with a wavelet. The bottom left is relative acoustic impedance, the summed seismic on its own. The bottom right is the absolute answer, the relative result standing on a low-frequency model built at the well.

Move the well position control from one end of the line to the other. The seismic panel does not change, because the recording is the recording. The relative panel does not change either, because summing the trace uses no wells. Only the absolute panel moves, and it moves by hundreds of impedance units in the gas sand.

Compare the bottom two panels against the top left. The relative result has the body in the right place with the right shape and sits on no level at all, so the color scale under it is arbitrary. The absolute result has values on it, and how close those values are to the truth depends on where the well was put.

The model follows the structure control is the difference between a low-frequency model interpolated along mapped horizons and one interpolated flat in time. Turning it off leaves the model hanging level while the geology dips underneath it, which is what happens when a background is built without horizons. Watch the crest.

Common misconception

"The relative panel is the rough version and the absolute panel is the good one."

Both panels carry exactly the same information from the seismic, because both were made from the same summed trace. The absolute panel has more numbers on it, and every one of those extra numbers came from the well. Putting a level on a result adds units, not evidence. Which of the two is more useful depends on the question. A relative section is enough to find a body and compare it with its surroundings. An absolute one is needed before a value can be carried to a rock physics model or a volume calculation.

WWhy this matters

The thing most worth remembering

If you take one number away from this set, take the share in step 1. On this well, in this window, about 86 percent of an impedance value comes from the background and about 14 percent from the seismic.

That ratio is not unusual and it is not a criticism. An inversion is doing the job it is supposed to do. It puts the detail the seismic recorded onto a level the wells supplied. The product is more useful than either piece alone.

What follows from it is a way of reading results. Detail and shape came from the recording, so trust them the way you trust the seismic. The level came from wells, so trust it the way you trust the wells. Less far from them, more near them.

It also explains a thing that puzzles people. Two contractors invert the same volume and hand back different impedance levels, and both fit the seismic perfectly. They are not disagreeing about the data. They are disagreeing about the background, and the data has no opinion on that.

Module 7 turns all of this into a short list of questions you can ask about a result somebody hands you. The first one on the list comes from this page. Which wells built the background, and how far away are they.

CCheck yourself

Four questions. Wrong answers are the useful ones

  1. Roughly how much of an impedance number on this well came from the seismic?

    About one part in seven, or 14 percent. The level is around nine thousand and the wiggle around it is around fifteen hundred. The seismic recorded the wiggle. Everything else was carried in from a well. Step 1 measures both, and the share hardly changes whatever you do to the survey.

  2. Why can't better processing recover the missing low frequencies?

    They were never recorded. Sources do not produce them and receivers do not sense them, so there is nothing in the data to bring out. Processing can only rearrange what was recorded. This is different from something being buried in noise, which is at least present.

  3. You swap the background for one from a well further away. What happens?

    The shape holds up and the numbers move. The shape came from the seismic, which you did not change. The level came from the well, which you did. This is why comparing one part of a section with another is safer than quoting a value. Both parts share whatever background was used, so the comparison survives an error that the value does not.

  4. An inversion fits the seismic almost perfectly. What does that tell you about the impedance values?

    Nothing at all. Every background on this page gives an answer that fits the seismic identically well, because changing the background does not touch the part the fit is measuring. A good fit tells you the seismic part is being explained. It is blind to the part that came from the wells, which is most of the number.

If question 1 surprised you

That is the reaction the module is for. Go to step 1 and read the two sizes: about 9,400 for the low-frequency component and about 1,500 for the seismic band. Then drag the lowest-frequency control across its whole range and watch the share stay where it is.

MHow this page works

The arithmetic, and what it leaves out

Everything on this page is computed while you watch, from a real log. This is the first module in the set to use one.

The well. Maui-4, drilled in the Taranaki Basin in 1970. The impedance curve is velocity from the sonic times density from the density log. The window runs from the depth you set over about 400 m, sampled to a regular time axis.

The split. The low-frequency component is a running average of the log, wide enough to pass only frequencies below the one you set. The seismic-band component is whatever is left over. The two add back to the original exactly.

The share. The low-frequency component is measured by its average value, about 9,400 here, because that is what it contributes to a number read off the section. The seismic-band component is measured by how far it typically swings about zero, around 1,500. The share is the first divided by the sum of the two. Those are two different kinds of measurement, a level against a swing, and the comparison is deliberate. The question being asked is how much of one printed impedance value came from each source, not how much variance each accounts for. Most of the numerator is zero frequency, which no survey records at all, so the share comes out near 86 percent across the whole range of the frequency control. That is the point of the control.

The distant wells are stand-ins. Rather than a second well, the page builds backgrounds from other depth intervals of this same well. They differ from one another by about 3 to 6 percent of the impedance level, which makes the effect in step 3 a mild one. At the default window the error rises by about 12 percent across the four. A genuinely different well in a different part of a basin would move it further. What the page demonstrates is the direction and the split, not the size.

The four intervals are spread across whatever room the log leaves above or below the window you have set, rather than sitting at fixed offsets, because the logged section is only about 320 m longer than the window itself. The distance to each one is printed beside the control, and it changes as the window moves.

Shape is scored against the log filtered to the band the wavelet carries, using a zero-phase filter. That is the fair test. The seismic never held the lowest or the finest parts of the log, so asking the summed trace to reproduce them would be measuring the wrong thing. Scored that way the agreement comes out above 0.9, and the number to watch is how completely it ignores which well supplied the background.

Where this page simplifies.

  • The inversion is a sum plus a background, not the loop a contractor runs. The loop gets a similar answer with more machinery, and it needs the same background.
  • The wavelet is known exactly. Estimating it is a job of its own and errors in it make everything on this page worse.
  • One trace, one well. A real background model is interpolated between wells along horizons, which is a piece of interpretation in its own right and can be done well or badly.
  • The 86 percent is this well, this window. A shallower target, a slower section or a larger contrast would shift it. The order of magnitude is what to remember, not the digits.
  • No noise and no angles. Both belong to the advanced set.

Instructor notes
Learning objectives
  • Split an impedance log into the part a survey records and the part it does not.
  • State roughly what share of an impedance value comes from the background.
  • Explain why processing cannot recover the missing frequencies.
  • Say which conclusions survive a wrong background and which do not.
Summary

Most of an impedance number comes from wells, not from the seismic. Shapes and comparisons survive a wrong background; quoted values do not.

Suggested discussion questions
  • Your survey has one well in the corner and the target is 20 km away. What does that do to the numbers on the impedance map, and what would you still be willing to say?
  • A contractor shows a fit of 98 percent to the seismic. What has that actually demonstrated?
  • If most of the answer comes from wells, why bother inverting at all?
Common misconceptions this page addresses
  • That better processing could recover the low frequencies.
  • That a good fit to the seismic means the impedance values are right.
Approximate completion time

Twenty minutes. The share readout in step 1 is the moment worth pausing on; students are usually surprised, and the surprise is the lesson.
