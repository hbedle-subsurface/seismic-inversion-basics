Module 2  ·  A number about a rock

An amplitude describes a boundary. An impedance describes a rock.

Impedance turned up in module 1 as a step in the arithmetic. Here it gets looked at on its own. One number belongs to a rock. The other belongs to the contact between two rocks. That difference is why anyone converts a seismic volume into impedance in the first place.

Three short steps. Step 2 is the one to spend time on.

1A value at every depth, or a value at every boundary

Change either rock and watch both panels

A well log gives a number at every depth. Impedance is that kind of number. Each rock has one, whether or not anything is next to it. A rock sitting on its own still has an impedance.

A reflection coefficient is not that kind of number. It exists only where two rocks meet, and it is made from the difference between them. One rock on its own has no reflection coefficient, because there is nothing for it to differ from.

The left panel shows both rocks and their impedances. The right panel shows what happens at the contact. Change the lower rock until the two impedances match and watch the reflection vanish, even though there is still a real geological contact there.

Common misconception

"If there is a geological boundary, there will be a reflection."

Set the lower rock to 2,400 m/s and 2.35 g/cc, the same as the upper rock. The reflection goes to zero. Now set it to 2,750 m/s and 2.05 g/cc. That is a different velocity, a different density and a different rock, and the impedance still comes to about the same number, so there is still almost no reflection. A real contact can be invisible on seismic, and a sand can sit against a shale with nothing to show for it.

2The same wiggle from different rocks

Move the multiplier and watch the two traces stay identical

The second pair of rocks on the control panel is your pair with both impedances multiplied by the same number. Set the multiplier to 1.8 and the second pair is 80 percent harder than the first, top and bottom.

Those are plainly different rocks. The panels show the two pairs side by side, with the trace each one produces. The traces are identical, and the readouts give the difference between them, which stays at zero however far you move the multiplier.

The reason is in the arithmetic. A reflection coefficient is the difference in impedance divided by the sum. Multiply both impedances by the same number and the difference and the sum both grow by that number, so the answer does not change at all. An amplitude tells you about the ratio between two rocks, not about either one.

Why this matters

Amplitude maps are everywhere in interpretation, and this is their limit. A bright spot says two rocks differ by a large ratio. It does not say which two rocks, or what either of them is. An impedance volume answers a question an amplitude map cannot: what is this rock.

3Finding the sand

Same layers, two displays. Try each one

Here is a stack of six layers with one soft sand in it. The left panel is the trace a survey would record. The right panel is the impedance of the same rock. The sand is in the same place on both.

On the trace, look for the sand. You will find two wiggles, one at its top and one at its base, with nothing between them, and the same kind of pair at every other contact in the section. Nothing marks out which pair is the sand.

Now drag the wavelet frequency control from one end to the other. The sand is 24 ms thick. At 15 Hz its top and its base are well inside one wavelet of each other, so their reflections overlap and what arrives is a single event. The sand is one wiggle, and nothing about that wiggle says how thick the bed under it is. Drag up past about 45 Hz and the same two reflections pull apart into two events a reader could pick separately.

While that happens, watch the impedance panel beside it. The block does not change at all — same top, same base, same value, at every frequency. The trace is showing the rock through a wavelet and the wavelet is what changed. Module 3 takes this apart properly; here it is the sharpest reason to want the right-hand display.

On the impedance, the sand is a block that sits lower than everything around it. You can point at it, measure how thick it is, and read a number off it that could be compared with a well. Move the how soft the sand is control and watch what each display does.

Common misconception

"The brightest reflection is the most interesting rock."

Leave the sand at its default softness and read the strongest-wiggle line below. It names the top of the limestone, not the sand, because the limestone sits against a much bigger contrast than the sand does. The sand only wins once you soften it past about a third. Amplitude ranks contacts by how different the two rocks are, not by how much anyone cares about them. That mismatch is the problem inversion exists to fix.

Why this matters

A geologist thinks in layers with properties: this is a sand, it is fifteen meters thick, it has this porosity. An amplitude section shows contacts between layers, which is the wrong shape of information for that question. Impedance is the right shape, which is why turning one into the other is worth the trouble.

WWhy this matters

What the module is for

Three things follow from impedance being a property of a rock.

It can be tied to a well. A well gives velocity and density at every depth, so it gives impedance at every depth. An inverted volume and a well log hold the same quantity and can be laid side by side. An amplitude and a well log do not.

It can be mapped and compared. An impedance value in one place means the same thing as an impedance value in another. Amplitudes depend on what happens to sit next to what, so comparing them across a survey is harder than it looks.

It can be turned into geology. Porosity and lithology relationships are fitted against impedance at wells, and then applied to an impedance volume. That sequence does not start from an amplitude.

None of this makes amplitude useless. It is what was recorded, and everything else is derived from it. The point is that it answers a question about pairs of rocks when the question you usually have is about one rock.

CCheck yourself

Four questions. Wrong answers are the useful ones

  1. A sandstone has an impedance of 6,200. What does that number describe?

    The sandstone itself. Impedance belongs to a rock the way porosity or density does. It has a value whether or not there is anything next to it. The contact at its top has a different kind of number, a reflection coefficient, and that one needs two rocks before it means anything.

  2. Pair A is 4,000 over 6,000. Pair B is 8,000 over 12,000. How do their reflections compare?

    Exactly the same, at 0.200 each. The reflection coefficient is the difference divided by the sum. For pair A that is 2,000 over 10,000. For pair B it is 4,000 over 20,000. Both come to one fifth. Doubling both rocks doubled the difference and the sum together, so nothing changed. Step 2 lets you slide this and watch the two traces stay on top of each other.

  3. Two rocks are geologically different but happen to have the same impedance. What does the seismic show at the contact?

    Nothing at all. The wave carries straight on as though the contact were not there. This happens in real sections. A sand can sit against a shale with no reflection to mark it, because a faster rock that is also less dense can end up with the same impedance as a slower, denser one. The geology is still there. The seismic just has nothing to say about it.

  4. Why would an interpreter want an impedance volume rather than the amplitude volume it was made from?

    It describes layers rather than contacts. It does not hold more data, and it does not have better resolution. The impedance was made from the amplitude, so it cannot contain anything the amplitude did not have, and later modules show it actually needs help from outside the seismic. What changes is the shape of the information. Layers with values are what a geologist can tie to a well, map, and turn into porosity.

If question 2 surprised you

Go to step 2 and move the multiplier slowly from 0.5 to 2.5 with the trace-difference readout in view. It stays at zero the whole way. That single fact is the reason this module exists.

MHow this page works

The arithmetic, and what it leaves out

Everything on this page is computed while you watch, in your browser. The functions are in assets/physics.js and each is a few lines long.

The arithmetic. Impedance is velocity times density. The reflection coefficient at a contact is the difference in impedance divided by the sum. The second pair in step 2 is made by multiplying both impedances by the slider value, which is why its reflection is identical to the first pair's. The multiplier cancels top and bottom.

The layer stack in step 3 is six layers of plausible clastic-section impedances with a soft sand near the middle and a hard limestone at the base. The values are made up rather than taken from a well, because a made-up section can be changed while you watch.

Where this page simplifies.

  • The wave arrives straight down. Real reflections change with angle, which is a whole subject of its own and is not needed here.
  • The wavelet is symmetrical and known exactly. Real wavelets are neither.
  • No noise, no multiples, no absorption. Nothing arrives that did not reflect once.
  • Impedance here is the acoustic one, made from P velocity and density. Rocks also have a shear impedance, which carries different information and belongs in the advanced set.
  • Getting an impedance from a real trace is much harder than reading it off this page. Modules 3 to 6 are about why.
  • The impedance axis in step 2 is fixed and does not stretch to hold everything. Hard rock at a large multiplier runs off the top of it, and the panel says so rather than pinning the curve against the edge and letting it look as though it had stopped moving. The reflection coefficients and the traces are unaffected: they are what the step is about, and they never leave their own axes.
Instructor notes
Learning objectives
  • State that impedance is a property of one rock and a reflection coefficient is a property of a pair.
  • Show that scaling both impedances leaves the reflection unchanged.
  • Give a reason why a real geological contact might produce no reflection.
  • Say what an impedance volume allows that an amplitude volume does not.
Summary

An amplitude carries the ratio between two rocks. An impedance carries the rock. That is what an inversion converts one into the other for.

Suggested discussion questions
  • You map a bright amplitude across a prospect. List three things other than hydrocarbons that could cause it.
  • A well finds a good sand that produced no reflection. What happened, and what would you do differently on the next well?
  • Why can amplitudes be difficult to compare between two surveys, when impedances are easier?
Common misconceptions this page addresses
  • That a geological boundary always makes a reflection.
  • That the brightest reflection marks the most interesting rock.
Approximate completion time

Fifteen minutes. Step 2 is the one to do together if you are short of time: the multiplier moving while the traces stay identical is the whole argument in one gesture.
