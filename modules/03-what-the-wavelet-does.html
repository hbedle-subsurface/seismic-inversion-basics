Module 3  ·  What the wavelet does

The recording is the rock seen through a wavelet

Module 2 showed that impedance is the thing worth having, so why not read it off the trace? A survey puts a wavelet at each boundary rather than a spike, and once two boundaries are closer together than the wavelet is long, their reflections overlap. Thin a bed down and watch it happen.

Four short steps. Step 2 is the experiment worth doing slowly, and step 4 is a game.

1A bed has two boundaries

Leave the thickness at 30 m for now

A sand sitting in shale gives two reflections. One where the shale meets the top of the sand, and one where the base of the sand meets shale again. They have opposite signs, because the impedance steps down going in and back up coming out.

The left panel shows the rock and its impedance. The middle of the right panel shows the two reflections as sticks: one number each, at the depth each boundary sits at. The right of that panel shows the trace, with a wavelet laid on each stick.

At 30 m the bed is thick enough that the two wavelets land in different places and stay apart. You can see a top and a base, count them, and measure the gap between them.

Why this matters

Almost every reservoir you will care about is a bed inside something else, so this pair of reflections is the shape most interpretation is built on. How far apart they land, and whether they land apart at all, decides what you can measure from a seismic section.

2Now thin it down

Take the thickness slider from 60 m to 2 m, slowly

The rock keeps getting thinner all the way. The trace does not keep up.

At the settings the page opens with, the gap between the peak and the trough follows the real thickness of the bed down to about 13 m. Below that it stops. The two reflections have started to overlap, and the gap settles at roughly the separation between the wavelet's own peak and trough however thin the sand gets. The left panel plots the measured gap against the real thickness, and the readout prints the thickness where the two part company at whatever frequency is set.

The third readout turns that gap back into a thickness, which is what an interpreter does with it. Above the limit it comes out a little short. Below the limit it stops falling, so a 4 m sand is reported as something over 14 m thick.

The size of the wiggle keeps changing after the gap has stopped. It rises to a peak and then falls away toward nothing. So below that thickness a bright reflection may mean a thicker bed, or a bigger contrast, or neither, and the trace does not distinguish them.

The teal line is the quarter-wavelength rule of thumb that step 3 works with. It sits at a thicker bed than the point where this page's measurement actually parts company with the truth, by a factor of about 0.6 at every frequency. The rule is the cautious one, and the readout prints both numbers.

Common misconception

"A brighter reflection means a thicker bed."

Set the thickness to 15 m and note the size of the wiggle. Now set it to 40 m — a bed nearly three times thicker — and the wiggle is smaller. Below the resolution limit the two reflections add together and reinforce each other, and that adding peaks at one particular thickness rather than growing forever. A bright event can mean a thick bed, a thin one at just the right thickness, or a large contrast. The trace does not say which.

3A shorter wavelet sees more

Move the frequency and watch the limit move with it

The limit in step 2 came from the wavelet rather than from the rock. A higher frequency is a shorter wavelet, and a shorter wavelet keeps two reflections apart at thicknesses where a longer one cannot.

There is a rule of thumb for where the limit falls: a quarter of the wavelength of the wavelet in the rock. Wavelength is velocity divided by frequency, so the readouts work it out from the numbers you have set. The left panel shows the same bed recorded at three frequencies so you can see the limit move.

This is also why nobody can simply buy their way past the problem. Higher frequencies are absorbed by the earth as they travel, so a deep target arrives with a longer wavelet than a shallow one whatever the survey did. The limit is worse the deeper you look.

Why this matters

Geological thickness and seismic resolution are two different things, and confusing them is one of the more expensive mistakes available. A bed can be there, be thick enough to produce, and still not be separable on the section. When somebody says a reservoir is below seismic resolution, this is what they mean.

4Rank the beds

Pick the thickest of the three, then try the same deal at a higher frequency

Three sands are hidden in the shale. Each has its own thickness and its own impedance contrast, and all three are recorded with the wavelet set in the control panel. Only the traces are shown. Pick the thickest sand.

Choosing fills in the right panel: the thickness each trace implies, from the gap between its peak and its trough, beside the thickness that is actually there. Deal again, and try a few deals at 20 Hz and then a few at 60 Hz.

Two things are being scored. How often you name the thickest bed, and how often the thickness read off the trace is within a quarter of the truth for all three beds. The second one is the one that moves with frequency.

Why this matters

The wiggle that catches the eye first is the brightest one, and across a few hundred deals the brightest trace is the thickest bed about a third of the time, which is what picking at random would give. The separation between peak and trough is the measurement that ranks them. The thickness it reports is a different matter: below the limit it stops falling, so a 5 m sand and a 15 m sand are both reported at something over 15 m. A thickness map made this way still contours, still looks like a map, and over the thin part of the field the contours are the wavelet rather than the reservoir.

WWhy this matters

And what it means for the rest of the set

Module 2 argued that impedance is the thing worth having. This module is the reason you cannot simply read it off the trace.

The trace is the rock seen through a wavelet, and the wavelet throws information away. Two boundaries closer together than about a quarter of a wavelength arrive as one event, and the event that arrives does not carry enough to take them apart again. No amount of processing puts back what was not recorded.

That is one of the two reasons the inverse problem does not have a single answer. Many different beds produce the same wiggle once they are thin enough, so a trace on its own cannot choose between them. Module 4 makes that concrete by having you build two of them.

The other reason is the opposite end of the scale: a survey records no very low frequencies, so the slow variation of impedance with depth is missing as well. Module 6 is about where that comes from.

CCheck yourself

Four questions. Wrong answers are the useful ones

  1. A sand sits inside shale. How many reflections does it produce?

    Two, with opposite signs. Impedance steps down going into a soft sand and back up coming out of it, so the two reflections have opposite polarity. If the sand were harder than the shale the pair would flip, but they would still be opposite to each other.

  2. You thin the bed from 30 m to 5 m. What happens to the gap you can measure between the peak and the trough on the trace?

    It shrinks, then stops. Down to a thickness set by the wavelet the measured gap follows the real thickness. Below that the two reflections overlap and the gap settles at roughly the separation between the wavelet's own peak and trough, no matter how thin the bed gets. Step 2 plots this, and the thickness where the line leaves the diagonal is the resolution limit.

  3. Two events on a section are separated by a bright, strong reflection. Which is the safest reading?

    A large contrast, or a thickness that happens to make the two reflections reinforce. Brightness below the resolution limit depends on thickness as much as on the rock, which is why a 15 m bed on this page gives a bigger wiggle than a 40 m one. Hydrocarbons are one thing that can raise a contrast, but so can lithology, porosity, cement and the processing. Amplitude on its own does not choose between them.

  4. Your target is at 4,000 m and the wavelet there is about 20 Hz. The rock velocity is 3,000 m/s. Roughly how thin a bed can you separate?

    About 40 m. The wavelength is velocity divided by frequency, so 3,000 over 20 is 150 m, and a quarter of that is about 37 m. Anything thinner arrives as a single event. This is worth doing in your head before you promise anyone a thickness map: at depth, with low frequencies and fast rock, the limit can be larger than the reservoir.

If question 3 surprised you

Go to step 2 and compare the wiggle at 15 m with the wiggle at 40 m, with the size readout in view. The thinner bed gives the bigger wiggle. Seeing that once is worth more than reading it several times.

MHow this page works

The arithmetic, and what it leaves out

Everything on this page is computed while you watch, in your browser, from the numbers in the control panel. The functions are in assets/physics.js.

The model. Shale at 3,000 m/s and 2.45 g/cc, with a sand of the velocity and density you set. The thickness slider is a real thickness in meters; the page converts it to time using the sand's own velocity, so changing the sand velocity moves the base of the bed as well as its impedance.

The measured gap is the separation in milliseconds between the largest peak and the largest trough on the trace. That is a crude way to measure a bed and it is chosen on purpose, because it is what an interpreter does with a cursor.

Where the gap stops following is measured rather than assumed. The page sweeps the bed from 2 m to 60 m in half-meter steps at the frequency you have set. At each thickness it compares the measured gap with the true two-way time, and it reports the thickest bed at which the two differ by more than 20 percent. Both extremum positions are fitted through their neighboring samples, so the gap carries a fraction of a millisecond rather than jumping a whole sample at a time.

The rule of thumb drawn on the step 2 panel is a quarter of a wavelength, calculated from the sand velocity and the wavelet's peak frequency. On this page the measured departure lands at about 0.6 of that value at every frequency. The rule is built on the peak frequency, while the thing that sets the plateau is the separation between the wavelet's peak and its trough. The rule and the measurement scale together with velocity over frequency; they differ by a constant. The thickness at which the wiggle is brightest is different again, and the page reports each of the three rather than treating them as one number.

The ranking game in step 4 draws three thicknesses between 4 m and 45 m, at least 3 m apart. Each bed then gets its own density between 1.90 and 2.40 g/cc, which is a range of impedance contrast of roughly two and a half to one. All three share the velocity in the control panel, so one assumed velocity converts every gap into a thickness. The only things that vary between the beds are thickness and contrast. Contrast has to vary: three beds that differed only in thickness could be ranked from amplitude alone, which is not the position an interpreter is in. The deal number is the seed, so a deal is reproducible and the link at the top of the page carries it.

What step 4 does and does not show. The two reflections from a bed in uniform shale are equal and opposite, so the shape of the trace depends on the thickness alone and the contrast only scales it. The peak-to-trough separation therefore still puts three beds in the right order here however thin they are, and tools/check-game.js confirms that across 600 deals. What fails below the limit is the number, not the ordering: the separation stops falling, so the thickness it reports is too large by as much as a factor of five. In a real section, with noise and with beds above and below, the ordering goes as well.

Where this page simplifies.

  • The wavelet is symmetrical and the same at all depths. Real wavelets lose their high frequencies as they travel, so a deep target is recorded with a longer wavelet than a shallow one — which makes the limit worse with depth, not better.
  • The wave arrives straight down and there is no noise. Noise makes a weak thin-bed response harder to see and so makes the practical limit worse than the one measured here.
  • One bed in a uniform shale. Real sections have beds above and below whose reflections also overlap, which is messier than this and no easier.
  • Resolution here is vertical only. There is a horizontal limit too, set by the migration and the Fresnel zone, and it is usually worse.
  • The quarter-wavelength rule is a rule of thumb, not a law. Different definitions of resolution give somewhat different numbers, and careful work uses the measurement rather than the rule.
Instructor notes
Learning objectives
  • State that a bed produces two reflections of opposite sign.
  • Describe what happens to the measured gap as a bed thins below the resolution limit.
  • Calculate a quarter-wavelength limit from a velocity and a frequency.
  • Give two reasons other than thickness for a bright reflection.
  • Explain why three beds cannot be ranked by thickness once they fall below the limit.
Summary

Geological thickness and seismic resolution are different things. Below a thickness set by the wavelet the trace stops reporting thickness and starts reporting a mixture of thickness and impedance contrast.

Suggested discussion questions
  • Your reservoir is 12 m thick at 3,500 m depth. What would you need to see it as two events, and is that realistic?
  • An amplitude map shows a bright patch. List four things that could produce it.
  • If a bed is below resolution, is the seismic useless over it? What can you still say?
  • In step 4 the three beds differ in contrast as well as thickness. What would the game be like if they all had the same contrast, and why would that be the easier problem?
Common misconceptions this page addresses
  • That a brighter reflection means a thicker bed.
  • That resolution is a property of the rock rather than of the wavelet.
  • That a thickness map is a measurement of thickness everywhere on it.
Approximate completion time

Twenty-five minutes. Step 2 rewards being done slowly: drag the thickness from 60 m down to 2 m while watching the gap readout, and stop at the moment it stops changing. Step 4 works well as a class exercise, with the frequency at 20 Hz for the first few deals and 60 Hz for the next few, and the two scores read out at the end.
