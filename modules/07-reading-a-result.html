What the display is good for, and what to ask about it

1Contacts above, layers below

The same line, displayed two ways

The upper panel is the amplitude section. A wiggle wherever the rock changes, two of them for a body with a top and a base, and nothing in between. The lower panel is the impedance section made from the same recording, where the body is a block of color and the wiggles have become its edges.

This is the plain case for inversion, and it is worth looking at before any of the cautions. A geologist thinks in layers with properties. An amplitude section shows the contacts between layers. An impedance section shows the layers.

Set the body softness to zero and the body disappears from both displays together, leaving the bedding of the real well that the section was built on. Neither display invents anything the other does not have. What changes between them is the arrangement of the information, not its amount.

Where the boundary actually is. Both panels carry a dashed line at the top of the body, and it is worth checking what each display puts there. On the impedance section the line falls on an edge: the color changes at the boundary, because impedance is a property of a layer and the layer starts there. On the amplitude section the same line falls in the middle of a dark band, halfway down the event rather than at the top of it.

That is what a zero-phase wavelet does. The wavelet is symmetric about its own center, so when a copy of it is laid on the reflection at the boundary, the largest excursion sits at the boundary and the side lobes sit above and below. The event is roughly a wavelength across and the interface is at its middle, not at the point where the trace first leaves the zero line. Picking the top of the black band puts the horizon half a lobe shallow, which at 30 Hz in this section is around 8 ms, or about 20 m.

Why this matters

Almost every question a geologist puts to a seismic section is about a layer: how thick, how porous, what rock. An amplitude display answers a different question, about the contacts. Turning one into the other is what all of the trouble in modules 3 to 6 was for.

2The same recording, inverted twice

Move the background control and compare

Two sections, from one recording, with backgrounds built from wells in different places. Look at them before reading any numbers.

They are hard to tell apart. The body is in the same place, the same shape and the same size on both, because all of that came from the seismic and the seismic did not change. Then read the values. The body's impedance differs between the two by the amount in the readout.

That is module 6's result, seen on a display instead of a curve. It gives a practical rule with two halves. Comparing one part of a section with another is sound, because both parts carry the same background. Quoting a value and taking it somewhere else. To a rock-physics relationship, to a core measurement, to a different survey. Depends on a background that somebody built and that nobody measured at your target.

Use the pointer. Move the mouse across either section and a crosshair appears at the same trace and the same time on both. The readouts underneath give the impedance each panel holds at that exact spot, and how far apart they are. Comparing two displays by eye across a gap is how a difference like this gets missed on a real screen.

Common misconception

"The color bar has numbers on it, so the numbers are measured."

Most of every number on that bar was carried in from a well, as module 6 measured. About 86 percent of it. Both sections here fit the seismic identically well, because changing the background does not touch the part that fits. The color bar is a measurement wearing a borrowed level. The pattern is yours to trust; the level belongs to whoever built the background.

3The questions worth asking

Five modules, on one card

Somebody hands you an impedance volume. These are the questions that decide how much of it you can use, in the order worth asking them.

  • How were the wells tied, and what was the tie hung on? Every log that went into this result reached it through a tie. A tie that is a cycle out, or one corrected with a bulk shift where the sonic needed a stretch, moves everything downstream by the same amount and looks consistent while doing it. Module 5.
  • Which wells built the background, and how far away are they? Most of every number came from there rather than from the seismic. The further the wells, the softer the values, while the shapes stay just as good. Module 6.
  • What is the wavelet, and how thin a bed can it separate? A quarter of a wavelength, roughly. Anything thinner arrives as one event, and its brightness then mixes thickness with contrast. Ask before promising a thickness map. Module 3.
  • What else would have fitted this data? The display shows one model. Others fit too, and something other than the seismic chose between them. Ask what that something was. Module 4.
  • Am I comparing, or quoting? Comparing one part of the section with another is sound. Taking a value somewhere else depends on the background. The same section supports the first and not always the second. Modules 4 and 6.
  • Does the simplest method agree with it? Summing the trace takes seconds and needs no well information. If its shape matches the delivered result, then the difference between the two is the background model. That is exactly the part to ask about. Modules 1 and 6.

The panels put the last one into practice. On the left is the delivered impedance section. On the right is the summed trace, made from the recording alone. Compare the shapes.

Why this matters

None of this is a reason to distrust an inversion. It is a description of what one is: a careful estimate built from a recording and a set of stated assumptions. Knowing which assumptions is what turns a picture into evidence.

WWhy this matters

What the whole set was for

An impedance section is the closest thing seismic gives a geologist to a picture of rock. Layers instead of contacts, values instead of amplitudes, and edges that sit where the rock changes. Step 1 is the honest case for the technique and it is a strong one.

Most of what an inversion delivers is reliable. The shapes are reliable. Comparisons within one section are reliable. Those are the things most interpretation actually needs, and they held up under everything the earlier modules measured.

What needs care is the part that came from outside the seismic. The level on the color bar, the detail the display implies, and the single answer shown where several fit. All three are measurable, and the five questions in step 3 are how you find out.

The habit worth taking away is small. When somebody shows you an impedance section, ask which wells built the background and how thin a bed the wavelet can separate. Two questions, half a minute, and you will know most of what you need about how far to trust the numbers on it.

CCheck yourself

Five questions, drawing on the whole set

  1. What does an impedance section show that an amplitude section does not?

    Layers rather than contacts. Not more detail. The impedance was made from the amplitude and cannot hold anything the amplitude did not. And certainly not fewer assumptions. It needed a background model the amplitude never asked for. What changed is the arrangement, and the arrangement is what a geologist can use.

  2. Two inversions of the same data, with backgrounds from different wells. What differs between the results?

    The values, not the pattern. The pattern came from the seismic, which did not change. The values came from the wells, which did. Step 2 lets you flip between the two sections and see how hard they are to tell apart while the readouts show the body's impedance moving.

  3. An inversion fits the seismic to 98 percent. What has that shown?

    Only that the seismic part is explained. Changing the background does not change how well the answer fits the seismic at all, so the fit cannot see a background error. A high fit is necessary and it is not evidence about the values. Only a well can check those, and the well is usually where they came from.

  4. Your target is 14 m thick, the rock is 3,000 m/s and the wavelet is 25 Hz. What can you say about thickness from the section?

    It is below resolution. The wavelength is 3,000 over 25, which is 120 m, and a quarter of that is 30 m. A 14 m bed arrives as a single event whose brightness depends on thickness and contrast together. That does not mean nothing can be said. The bed is clearly there, and its extent can be mapped. But a thickness read off the display would not be a measurement.

  5. You have ten minutes with an impedance volume and one question to ask. Which is the most useful?

    Which wells built the background. That single answer tells you how far to trust every number on the color bar, because most of each number came from there. The fit is nearly always high and tells you little. The software matters least of all.

MHow this page works

The arithmetic, and what it leaves out

Everything on this page is computed while you watch. The section is built from the Maui-4 impedance log and inverted trace by trace, so both displays come from the same recording.

How the section is built. A window of the log is used as the background geology and repeated across 48 traces with a gentle dip. A body is placed in it, softened by the amount you set and tapered at its edges so it thins rather than stopping at a wall. Each trace is forward modeled with the wavelet, then inverted.

Because the section carries a real well's bedding, it never goes completely quiet. With the body softness at zero there is still about 530 impedance units of contrast across that interval, which is the well's own layering rather than anything this page added.

The inversion is a sum plus a background, as in module 6. A contractor runs a loop instead, which gets a similar answer with more machinery and needs the same background. The loop belongs to the companion set.

The color scale is fixed across both sections in step 2 and does not rescale when the background changes. Software usually rescales each display to its own range by default, which would hide the very difference that step is about.

Where this page simplifies.

  • The section is a model, not a survey. No migration, no velocity error, no multiples, and every trace sits exactly where it belongs. A real line is harder to read than this one for reasons that have nothing to do with inversion.
  • The wavelet is known exactly and is symmetrical. In practice it is estimated, and errors in it move picks and values.
  • The distant well is a stand-in, built from another interval of the same log, so the shift in step 2 is a mild version of what a genuinely different well would do.
  • Lateral smearing is only the wavelet's width across the line. Real lateral resolution is set by the migration and the Fresnel zone and is worse.
  • No noise and no angles. Both belong to the companion set.

Instructor notes
Learning objectives
  • State what an impedance display shows that an amplitude display does not.
  • Predict what changes and what does not when the background model changes.
  • Say what a good fit to the seismic does and does not demonstrate.
  • Ask at least three sensible questions about a delivered impedance volume.
Summary

Trust the pattern and the comparisons. Ask where the level came from. Two questions — which wells, and how thin a bed the wavelet resolves — cover most of it.

Suggested discussion questions
  • A consultant presents an impedance map with a porosity scale on it. What would you need to see before using those porosities in a volume calculation?
  • When is it fair to compare impedance values between two different surveys, and when is it not?
  • Which of the five questions in step 3 would be hardest to get a straight answer to, and why?
Common misconceptions this page addresses
  • That numbers on a color bar are measurements.
  • That a high fit to the seismic validates the impedance values.
Approximate completion time

Twenty minutes, and step 3 works well as a printed card. The comparison in step 2 is worth doing on a projector: flipping the background while the sections look identical makes the point faster than any explanation.
