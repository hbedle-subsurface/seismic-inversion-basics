Module 5  ·  Tying a well

The logs are in depth. The seismic is in time.

A well is measured down a hole, in meters. A survey is recorded at the surface, in seconds. Nothing that uses both can happen until somebody works out which depth belongs to which time. This module is about why that has to be done and what an inversion needs from it. It is a short one.

1Even meters are not even milliseconds

Move the window and watch the spacing on the right change

Both panels hold the same impedance log from Maui-4. The left one is plotted against depth, which is how the well was measured. The right one is plotted against two-way time, which is where the seismic lives. The dashed rules run across both at the same places in the rock, so a bed can be followed from one panel to the other.

The rules are evenly spaced in depth, every 100 m. On the time panel they are not evenly spaced, because a wave crosses 100 m of one rock faster than 100 m of another. The readouts name the slowest and the fastest hundred meters in the window and say where each one is. They differ by a few percent, and the slow one is not reliably the shallow one: this well has a slow interval around 2,100 m with faster rock above and below it.

That is the whole problem in one picture. There is no single number that turns the left axis into the right one. Converting between them needs a velocity at every depth, and a sonic log is exactly that. Adding up the travel time across each log sample, from the top of the window downward, gives a two-way time for every depth. That list is the time-depth curve.

The coal at 2056 m is marked on both panels. It is 1.3 m thick and has the strongest impedance contrast in this well, which makes it the bed an interpreter would reach for when tying this log.

Common misconception

"You can convert depth to time by dividing by a velocity."

Only if the velocity is the same everywhere, and it never is. Read the slowest and the fastest hundred meters in this window: they differ by a few percent across a few hundred meters of one well, and moving the window moves both numbers. Depth to time needs a velocity at every depth, not a velocity for the section.

2Slide the well until it fits

Move the tie control until the two traces line up

The left panel has two traces on it. The gray one is the seismic recorded at the well. The red one is the synthetic seismogram: impedance from the logs, reflection coefficients from the impedance, a wavelet convolved onto them. That is module 1's calculation, run on a real log and placed on the time axis from step 1.

They start out in the wrong place. Adding up the sonic gives the time across the window and says nothing about the time down to the top of it, so the whole synthetic sits some tens of milliseconds off until somebody puts it where it belongs. Move the tie control until the strong coal event on the synthetic lands on the strong event in the seismic. The readout gives the correlation between the two traces, and there is a setting where it goes close to 1.

That is a well tie. Everything else about ties is refinement of it. A checkshot measures travel time directly at a few depths, which pins down that starting time properly instead of leaving it to be found this way. A stretch applied across the window handles the fact that a sonic reads the rock as slightly fast and the sum accumulates the error. Neither changes what the tie is or what it is for.

The right panel is what the tie produces: the time-depth curve, now anchored. It is read in both directions. A depth in the well gives a time on the seismic, and a time on the seismic gives a depth in the ground.

Why this matters

A tie is the one thing on a project that connects a measurement of the rock to a recording of the seismic. Until it exists, a well and a survey are two datasets about the same ground with no way to compare them.

3What an inversion needs from it

Move the tie away from where you left it and watch the readouts

An inversion needs three things from the tie, and it needs all of them before it can start.

The logs, on the seismic's time axis. Module 6 builds a low-frequency model by filtering impedance logs and spreading them across a survey. Those logs arrive through the tie. A log placed at the wrong time builds a model that is wrong at that time, and the inversion adds it to the answer without ever checking it.

The wavelet. A wavelet is estimated by comparing the log's reflection coefficients against the recorded trace at the well. That comparison only means anything once the two are on the same time axis, so the tie comes first, and a wavelet estimated from a poor tie carries the error into everything the inversion does with it.

A depth for every time. An inversion returns impedance against two-way time. Turning that into a depth, a thickness or a volume goes back through the tie.

The panels show the first of those. Move the tie control away from the value you found in step 2. The left panel puts the impedance log where your tie says it goes, against where it truly sits. The right panel is the low-frequency model built from each, which is what module 6 would be handed. The readouts give the depth the error is worth, the impedance you would report at the target, and the bed you would actually be looking at.

Ten milliseconds is about twenty meters of depth in this section. The tested oil sand at the top of the window is 21 m thick and the second oil sand below it is 9 m. A tie error of that size is enough to move a target out of the bed it belongs to. The coal makes the point hardest: it is 1.3 m thick with the lowest impedance anywhere in this window, and a tie a few milliseconds out reports the sandstone beside it instead.

Common misconception

"The tie is a formality. The inversion works on the seismic."

An inversion works on the seismic and on everything the wells supply, and module 6 measures the second of those at about 86 percent of the final number. All of it arrives through the tie. A tie that is out by ten milliseconds shifts the well-derived part of the answer by the same amount, everywhere, consistently — which is exactly the kind of error that does not look like an error on a map.

WWhy this matters

The whole module in one page

The problem. Wells are measured in depth. Seismic is recorded in two-way time. The two are not proportional to each other, because velocity changes from bed to bed.

The fix. A sonic log gives a velocity at every depth. Adding up the travel time across each sample gives a two-way time for every depth, which is the time-depth curve. Where that curve starts is settled by matching a synthetic seismogram against the seismic at the well, or, better, by a checkshot that measures the travel time directly.

What it delivers. Logs on the seismic's time axis, so they can be filtered into a low-frequency model. A comparison that lets a wavelet be estimated. And a depth for every time, so an inversion result can be turned into a thickness or a volume.

What goes wrong. An error in the tie moves everything downstream by the same amount and stays consistent while doing it, which is why it is hard to spot afterwards. Module 7 asks what to check about a delivered inversion, and how the wells were tied belongs near the top of that list.

CCheck yourself

Four questions. Wrong answers are the useful ones

  1. Why can a well not be placed on a seismic section by dividing its depths by one velocity?

    Because velocity changes from bed to bed. Step 1 gives the numbers for whatever window you have set: the slowest and the fastest hundred meters in it differ by a few percent. One velocity would place some beds correctly and the rest wrongly, and moving the window changes which ones.

  2. What does the sonic log contribute to the tie?

    A velocity at every depth. Adding up the travel time across each log sample gives a two-way time for every depth, which is the time-depth curve. The sonic does also go into impedance, alongside the density log, but that is a separate use of the same measurement.

  3. Adding up the sonic gives the time across the logged interval. What is still missing?

    The time down to the top of it. A sum has to start somewhere, and the sonic says nothing about the section above where the tool was run. That starting time comes from a checkshot, or is found by sliding the synthetic against the seismic until they agree, which is what step 2 has you do.

  4. A tie is ten milliseconds out. What does that do to an inversion built on it?

    It shifts the well-derived part, consistently. The logs reach the inversion through the tie, and module 6 measures the well-derived part at about 86 percent of the final number. A consistent shift is the hard kind of error to notice, because the result still looks orderly. Step 3 shows what ten milliseconds is worth in depth and in impedance.

MHow this page works

The arithmetic, and what it leaves out

Everything on this page is computed while you watch, from a real log.

The well. Maui-4, drilled in the Taranaki Basin in 1970. Velocity comes from the sonic and density from the density log; their product is the impedance curve. The window runs from the depth you set over about 400 m.

The time-depth curve. The travel time across each log sample is twice the sample spacing divided by the velocity there. Those are added from the top of the window downward, which gives the time across the window but not the time down to its top. This page starts that sum 18 ms early, so there is a real offset for the tie control to find.

The seismic at the well. Reflection coefficients from the impedance on the correct time axis, resampled to a 1 ms grid, convolved with a zero-phase Ricker wavelet at the frequency you set, with a small fixed amount of band-limited noise added. It is made the same way the synthetic is, so a good tie is possible and the exercise is about finding it.

The low-frequency model in step 3. A running average of the impedance log, wide enough to keep only what lies below the seismic band, computed on each of the two time axes.

What this leaves out

  • Drift. A real sonic reads the rock as slightly fast and the sum accumulates that error, so a tie usually needs a stretch across the window as well as a shift. One control here, because this module is about what a tie is for rather than how one is made.
  • The checkshot itself. It is described but not used as a control the student can move. The two-way time supplied with this log is a sonic integration anchored to the checkshot at the top of the interval, and over the 365 ms the log spans the integrated base differs from the checkshot by about 5 ms. That residual is left in rather than corrected, and it is small enough that nothing on this page turns on it.
  • Cycle skipping. A tie can settle a whole wavelet period away from the truth and still correlate well, which is the classic way a tie goes wrong. It does not happen on this log, because a long window with many reflectors of different strengths gives a sharply peaked correlation. It is worth knowing about anyway.
  • The wavelet is given. In practice the tie and the wavelet are estimated together, each depending on the other.
  • Phase. The wavelet here is zero-phase throughout. A real tie usually involves a phase rotation as well, and phase cannot be checked against the seismic alone.
  • One well, one trace. A survey is tied at every well available and the ties are checked against each other, which is where disagreements between them show up.

Instructor notes
Learning objectives
  • State why depth and two-way time are not proportional to each other.
  • Say what the sonic log contributes and what it cannot supply on its own.
  • Describe what a well tie is, in one sentence.
  • Name the three things an inversion needs from the tie.
Summary

The tie is what connects a well to a survey. Everything an inversion gets from wells arrives through it, and an error in it moves the answer consistently rather than visibly.

Suggested discussion questions
  • Which bed in a section you know would you hang a tie on, and why that one?
  • A contractor reports a tie correlation above 0.9. What would you still want to know?
  • If a tie is out by 10 ms, what does the depth map made from it look like, and would anybody notice?
Common misconceptions this page addresses
  • That depth converts to time with a single velocity.
  • That the tie is a formality because the inversion works on the seismic.
Approximate completion time

Ten to fifteen minutes. This is the shortest module in the set and is meant to be. Step 3 is the one to put on a projector: drag the tie away from its correct value and read the impedance readouts changing.
