The wavelet

1Four steps from rock to trace

Change a number on the left and watch what moves

Each panel below is calculated from the one before it.

Impedance is velocity multiplied by density. It is one number for each layer, and it describes the rock itself.

Reflection coefficients come from pairs of layers. Where two layers meet, the difference in their impedance decides how much of a passing wave comes back. Two boundaries here, so two reflections.

The trace is what a survey records. A seismic source does not make a sharp spike. It sends out a short burst of energy a few cycles long, called the wavelet, and the recording holds one copy of that shape for every reflection.

Common misconception

"A faster rock always gives a stronger reflection."

A reflection comes from the difference between two rocks, not from either one on its own. Set both layers to 3000 m/s and 2.30 g/cc and the reflection between them goes to zero, however fast they are. Try it. Put the same two numbers into layers 1 and 2 and watch the first reflection disappear from the trace.

Rock in, seismic out

Everything you did in step 1 ran the same way. You set the rock, and the seismic followed. That direction is called the forward problem. Given the rock, work out what the survey would record.

Why this matters

A survey never records the rock. It records what the rock did to a wavelet. Every seismic section you will ever look at has been through these four steps, and the wavelet is part of what you are seeing. Two surveys over the same ground with different wavelets produce different-looking sections.

3Now try it the other way

The rock is hidden. Only the trace is left

What to do here. The rock has been hidden. Move the three impedance controls until your red trace lies on top of the gray target trace in panel 3. When the two traces agree, work the fourth control: it multiplies all three of your impedances by the same number. Watch the trace score stay at 1.000 while the rock score walks away from zero. That is the whole point of the step.

In the field nobody hands you the rock. They hand you the trace and ask what is down there. The left panel shows only the trace, with the model hidden. See how far you get.

Some of it is easy. You can count the reflections and say how many boundaries there were. You can read the polarity and say which way the impedance stepped at each one, up or down.

Then it stops. The trace cannot tell you the impedance of layer 1, because a reflection is made from a difference and a difference does not carry the values that made it. Use the guess controls on the right panel to build a model, and the readouts will score how close your trace is to the target. More than one answer will fit.

More than one answer fitting is the property called non-uniqueness, and it is worth being concrete about what it means for a geologist. Suppose the middle layer comes back at 6,000 (m/s)(g/cc). Several rocks sit at that value:

  • A soft, organic-rich shale. Slow because of the clay and the kerogen in it, and not especially dense. A seal, or a source rock.
  • A gas-charged sand. Gas in the pore space drops the velocity sharply and the density with it. A reservoir.
  • A poorly cemented brine sand, high in porosity. Slow because the grains are barely held together. A reservoir with no gas in it.

On the trace those three are the same event. The impedance the inversion returns is the same number, the polarity is the same, and the amplitude is the same. Nothing further down the arithmetic separates them, because the difference between them was never recorded. Choosing among them is done with well control, with a rock physics model, with the shape of the body in map view, or with the angle information that a pre-stack inversion keeps — none of which is on this trace.

Common misconception

"If the traces match, I found the right rock."

Match the traces first, then take the multiplier from 0.5 to 2. Your trace does not move at all, because a reflection coefficient is a difference divided by a sum and multiplying both impedances scales the difference and the sum by the same amount. Meanwhile your rock walks thousands of units away from the truth. Watch the two scores separate. The trace can be right while the rock is wrong. That is the whole difficulty of inversion, and every later module is about handling it.

Why this matters

Working backward from the trace to the rock is what seismic inversion is. It is harder than the forward direction, not because the arithmetic is harder, but because more than one rock gives the same trace. An inversion result is therefore an estimate chosen from a set of possibilities, and the choosing is done partly by information that did not come from the seismic.

WWhy this matters

What the module is for

Two directions, and they are not equally difficult.

Forward. Rock to seismic. One answer, short arithmetic, no judgment needed. This is what a synthetic seismogram is, and it is how a well tie is made.

Backward. Seismic to rock. This is inversion. It has no single answer, and producing a useful one means adding information the survey never recorded.

Everything else in this set follows from that difference. Module 2 looks harder at impedance and why a geologist wants it. Module 3 shows what the wavelet does to a thin bed. Module 4 builds two different rocks that make the same trace. Module 5 puts a real well onto the seismic's time axis, module 6 is about where the extra information comes from, and module 7 is about what to ask when somebody hands you a result.

CCheck yourself

Four questions. Wrong answers are the useful ones

  1. Velocity goes up and density stays the same. What happens to impedance?

    Impedance is velocity multiplied by density. Multiply one of them by a larger number and the product gets larger. Try it on layer 2 and watch the impedance readout in step 1 follow the velocity you type.

  2. Two layers turn out to have exactly the same impedance. What does the boundary between them do to a passing wave?

    Nothing comes back. The reflection coefficient is the difference in impedance divided by the sum, and when the difference is zero so is the reflection. The wave carries straight on as though the boundary were not there. Remember this one. A real geological contact can be invisible on seismic if the two rocks happen to have the same impedance, and a sand can sit against a shale with no reflection to show for it.

  3. You have the trace but not the rock. Which of these can you read straight off it?

    The count of boundaries, and which way each one stepped. Values are a different matter. Each reflection came from a difference between two layers, and a difference does not carry the two numbers that made it. Knowing a step was +2,700 does not tell you whether it went from 4,200 to 6,900 or from 7,000 to 9,700.

  4. In step 3 you multiplied all three impedances by the same number and your trace did not change at all. What does that show?

    More than one rock makes the same trace. Each reflection is a difference in impedance divided by the sum of the two impedances. Multiplying every layer by 1.5 multiplies the difference by 1.5 and the sum by 1.5, so every reflection comes out identical and so does the trace. Nothing is broken and nothing was recorded badly. The information simply is not in the recording, and no amount of processing will put it there. Adding the same amount to every layer is a different matter: that changes the sums without changing the differences, so the trace does move a little. Module 4 takes both cases further.

If question 4 surprised you

Go back to step 3 and do it again with the two score readouts visible. The trace score holds at 1.000 while the rock score falls away. Seeing those two numbers separate is the single most useful thing in this module.

MHow this page works

The arithmetic, and what it leaves out

Everything on this page is computed while you watch, in your browser, from the six numbers in the control panel. There are no stored pictures and no server. The functions are in assets/physics.js and each one is a few lines long, so you can read the arithmetic the page describes.

The four steps. Impedance is velocity times density. A reflection coefficient is the difference in impedance across a boundary divided by their sum. The reflection coefficients are placed at the boundary times to make a reflectivity series. The trace is that series convolved with a Ricker wavelet of the frequency you set.

Where this page simplifies.

  • Flat layers and a wave arriving straight down. Real reflections change with angle, which is a whole subject of its own and is not needed to see the point here.
  • The wavelet is symmetrical and known exactly. Real wavelets are neither, and estimating one is a job in itself.
  • No noise, no multiples, no absorption, and no geometry. Nothing arrives that did not reflect once off a boundary you set.
  • Layer boundaries are placed in time, not depth. Converting between them needs the velocities and is left for later.
  • The scoring in step 3 compares your model with the true one, which you would never have in the field. It is here so the lesson can be measured rather than asserted. The trace score is one minus the difference between the two traces, measured in the target trace's own units, so it falls if your trace is the right shape at the wrong size. The rock score is the average difference in impedance across the three layers.
Instructor notes
Learning objectives
  • State that impedance is velocity times density and that it describes a rock.
  • State that a reflection comes from a difference between two rocks.
  • Distinguish the forward problem from the inverse problem.
  • Give one reason the inverse problem has more than one answer.
Summary

A seismic trace records differences between layers, not the layers themselves. That is why working backward from it does not have a single answer.

Suggested discussion questions
  • A sand and a shale give no reflection where they meet. Is the contact still there?
  • If two students hand in different models that both match the trace, how would you decide between them? What would you need that the seismic does not have?
  • Why might the same reservoir look different on two surveys shot ten years apart?
Common misconceptions this page addresses
  • That a faster rock reflects more strongly on its own.
  • That matching the data means the model is right.
Approximate completion time

Fifteen to twenty minutes on the three steps, plus five on the questions. Step 3 is the one worth doing together on a projector if you are short of time.
