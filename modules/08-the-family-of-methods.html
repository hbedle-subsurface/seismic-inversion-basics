Module 8  ·  The family of methods

The names on the menu, and what separates them

A software menu offers half a dozen kinds of inversion and rarely says what makes them different. Two questions sort almost all of it. What data goes in — the stacked volume, or the angles kept separate. How the algorithm works — one best answer, or many. Answer those two and you have four kinds of inversion, which is the whole beginner's map. Step 1 builds it, the next two show each division happening, and the names on a real menu come last, placed on the map rather than listed cold.

1Two questions sort the whole menu

Answer both and you have named a kind of inversion

Before any of the names, there are two questions. Everything on a software menu is an answer to both of them, and a beginner who can ask them can place a method they have never heard of.

Question one: what data goes in? A survey records each reflection many times, from source-receiver pairs at different distances, which means at different angles. Ordinary processing stacks those together into one average trace per location. A post-stack inversion uses that stacked volume. A pre-stack inversion keeps the angles separate, because how a reflection changes with angle carries information that averaging destroys.

Question two: how does the algorithm work? A deterministic inversion looks for the single model that best fits the recording and returns it. A stochastic one accepts that many models fit — which module 4 showed — and returns a whole set of them instead, so the spread between them can be counted.

Two questions with two answers each makes four kinds. Move the controls below the grid to choose an answer to each, and the cell you land on says what that combination returns, what it needs before it can be run, and what it is worth using for.

Common misconception

"The four kinds are four methods, so a piece of software does one of them."

They are two independent choices, not a list. A post-stack deterministic inversion is the ordinary case and a pre-stack stochastic one exists and is expensive; every combination on the grid is a real thing somebody sells. The names on a menu are not the four kinds. They are particular recipes, and each one sits in a cell of this grid. Step 4 puts the common ones where they belong.

4The names you will actually see

The differences are assumptions, not cleverness

Step 1 gave four kinds of inversion. The names below are not those four again. They are the recipes a menu actually offers, and every one of them lives in the top-left cell of that grid: post-stack and deterministic. They differ from each other in what they assume and what they need, not in which data goes in or how many answers come back.

That is worth being blunt about, because two sets of four are easy to confuse. Post-stack against pre-stack is a question about the data. Deterministic against stochastic is a question about the algorithm. Relative against absolute, which the paragraphs below take up, is a question about the output. The names here are the choices left over once all three are settled the ordinary way.

All four run on one trace from one well, so what you see between them is the method and nothing else.

Read the tags under the curves first. Three of the four say rel and one says abs, and that is the third question worth asking about any result: does it come with impedance values on the axis, or only a shape? It is not the same question as either of the two on the map, because it is about the output rather than the data or the algorithm, and any cell of the grid can produce either.

A relative result has the right shape and no absolute value. Summing the trace gives one, as module 1 had you do: the recording and the wavelet, and nothing else. No well logs go into it. What it cannot tell you is whether this rock sits at 6,000 or at 11,000, because a reflection records only the difference between two rocks.

An absolute result has impedance values on the axis. Getting there means adding a low-frequency model built from well logs, which module 6 measured at about 86 percent of the final number. Only model-based, of the four here, does that — which is why it is the only one tagged abs, and why its score is the one measured against the whole log rather than against the seismic band.

The thing to notice is what the background model did not do. It moved the curve along the impedance axis and left its shape alone. A background model says where a curve sits; it does not sharpen it, add detail, or make it follow the log more closely. So if the question is where a body is rather than what its impedance value is, a relative result has already answered it — which is the first thing to establish about any result somebody hands you.

Relative, or band-limited. Sum the trace. No inputs, no assumptions past the wavelet, no absolute value.

Colored. Pass the whole volume through a single filter and call the output impedance. The filter is designed once, by comparing seismic traces against impedance logs from wells in the area and working out what would have to be done to the one to make it resemble the other. After that it is applied everywhere without looking at a well again. It runs on a whole volume in minutes, which is why interpreters like it, and it is relative: no well logs go in per trace, so no absolute values come out.

Sparse spike. Assume the earth has only a few significant boundaries and find them. A real one deconvolves the wavelet out of the trace first, then searches for the smallest set of reflection coefficients that still reproduces the recording. The panel here takes a shortcut and keeps the largest samples of the trace itself, which shows the assumption at work without the machinery. The how many spikes control is that assumption, and it is somebody's choice.

Move it across its whole range and watch the score. It does not simply climb. Too few spikes throws away real layering and the answer goes blocky and wrong. Too many starts fitting the noise as though it were geology. There is a best setting in between, and nothing in the seismic tells you where it is. You would find it by trying values at a well.

Model-based. Start from a background model built from wells, adjust it until its synthetic matches the recording, and hand back the result. This is the workhorse and the one most commercial products mean by inversion. It is the only one of the four that returns absolute values.

The three panels below are the same synthetic line used in module 6: a low-relief anticline with a wedge sand across it and a sand at 412 ms holding gas over the crest.

The relative panel finds the gas sand and puts it in the right place with no wells at all, and its color bar has no units on it. The sparse-spike panel puts the same body back as a small number of blocky boundaries, which is easy to pick a horizon on and has lost the internal detail. The model-based panel has impedance values, and those values rest on a low-frequency model built at the well marked on the panels. All three are made from the same recording.

Move the how many spikes control while watching the sparse-spike panel. Too few and the section falls to a handful of thick blocks with the thin beds gone. Too many and the noise is being fitted along with the geology, and the score falls again.

Why the three scores are not compared with each other

Relative and sparse spike return only the band the survey recorded, so they are scored against the seismic-band part of the true impedance — the part they set out to recover. Model-based returns absolute values and is scored against the whole log. Scoring a relative result against a full log would count the missing low frequencies against a method that never claimed to have them, and would make it look poor when what it is is incomplete. The readout labels say which comparison each score is.

Why this matters

The differences between these methods are assumptions rather than accuracy. Sparse spike is not worse than the others; it answers a different question, one about where the significant boundaries are. Choosing a method means choosing which assumption you are willing to make, and that is a decision a geologist can take part in.

2Post-stack or pre-stack

The first division on the menu, and the one that decides what you get

Stacking adds together the traces that reflected from the same subsurface point at different source-receiver distances. It raises signal against noise, and it averages away the change of amplitude with angle. Post-stack inversion runs on that stacked volume. It is faster, needs less of everything, and returns acoustic impedance, which is one number about the rock.

Pre-stack inversion keeps the angles separate instead of averaging them. Amplitude changes with angle in a way that depends on more than acoustic impedance, so keeping the angles allows a second elastic property to be estimated alongside it: shear impedance, and from the two together the ratio Vp/Vs. It needs angle gathers, a wavelet for each angle stack, wells with a shear sonic, and more of everybody's time.

The panels show what that extra work makes possible. The line has two soft bodies in the same interval. On the left, near the crest, is the gas sand. On the right flank is a lens of soft organic shale. Their acoustic impedances are within a hundred units of each other, which is nothing.

On the acoustic impedance panel they are the same color. Anything read off that panel — a bright body, a soft anomaly, an impedance value carried into a rock physics model — says the same thing about both. On the shear impedance panel they are plainly different, because gas in the pore space drops the compressional velocity sharply and leaves the shear velocity almost alone. The shale has no gas in it and no reason to behave that way.

That is the case for pre-stack inversion in one picture. Not that it is more accurate, but that it answers a question acoustic impedance cannot be asked. Move the wavelet frequency and the two bodies stay just as hard to tell apart on the left and just as easy on the right — this is not a resolution problem and a better survey does not fix it.

Common misconception

"Pre-stack inversion is the better one, so use it if you can afford it."

It is not a better version of the same measurement, it is a different one. If the question is where a body is and how it compares with its surroundings, post-stack answers it and pre-stack takes more of everything to answer it no better. Pre-stack is the one to reach for when the question is what the rock is, or what is in its pores, which is the case on this panel. It also needs inputs that many projects do not have: angle gathers that survived processing in good order, and at least one well with a shear sonic.

3One answer, or a range of them

The second division on the menu

Everything so far returned one answer. That is called deterministic inversion, and it is what most projects use.

Module 4 showed that many models fit the same data. A stochastic inversion takes that seriously and returns a set of them instead of one, all fitting the recording equally well. The left panel draws as many as you ask for.

The value is not the individual curves. It is that you can count them. If eight realizations out of twelve put this interval below 8,000, that is a number you can carry into a decision, and it is a kind of number a single deterministic answer cannot give you at all.

It takes considerably more computing than a single answer, and the spread you get depends entirely on what the realizations were allowed to vary. Limits chosen badly produce a narrow, confident-looking spread that means very little.

Common misconception

"Stochastic inversion is more accurate."

It is not more accurate. It is more honest about what it does not know. Every realization on this page fits the recording as well as the deterministic answer does, and one of them is no more likely to be right than another. What you gain is the ability to say how wide the range is, and to count how much of it falls on one side of a number that matters to you.

5The whole menu

Every name you are likely to meet, and what it is for

You now have all three questions. What data goes in, from step 2. How many answers come back, from step 3. Whether the output carries impedance values, from step 4. Every name on the tables below is a combination of those three answers, and nothing more.

The first two questions are the ones worth carrying away, because between them they account for four names that turn up constantly. One is about the data that goes in, and step 2 showed what it decides. The other is about the algorithm that processes it, and step 3 showed what that decides.

Where to go beyond this. These four are the ones worth knowing before anything else. The methods a contractor actually runs are variations within them. The machinery that makes those work is a course of its own: wavelet estimation, the iterative model-based loop, building a background model across a survey, and the rock physics that turns an elastic property into a lithology. The companion set, How Seismic Inversion Actually Works, covers those on real well data. For the theory behind the angle dependence that pre-stack inversion uses, the standard references are the Zoeppritz equations and the linear approximations to them.

Two things in that table are worth reading twice. The low-frequency model column is required for every method that returns absolute values and for none of the methods that do not, which is the same division module 6 measured. And the wells column never reaches zero except in the first row: every method that puts numbers on a color bar got those numbers, in the end, from a well.

Choosing, in practice

There is no best method. The question is which one matches the data you have and the decision you are making. One well and a post-stack volume rules out most of the list. A question about fluid rather than lithology points at pre-stack. A decision that needs a probability rather than a value points at stochastic. And a quick look, before anybody is paid, is nearly always relative.

CCheck yourself

Six questions

  1. A contractor offers you a "pre-stack stochastic" inversion. Without knowing anything else about it, what two things does that name already tell you?

    The angles were kept separate, and it returns many models. That is all the two words mean, and it is enough to know that a second elastic property is available and that the spread between the models can be counted. It says nothing about whether it is accurate — a name on a menu never does. Every method sits in one cell of step 1's grid, and reading the name is reading the cell.

  2. What is the main difference between a relative and an absolute result?

    Relative has no values on the axis. Its shape is just as good — the curves in step 4 take their shape from the same recording, and the background model moves the model-based one along the axis without changing its form. What a relative result lacks is the level, which has to come from wells. Everything that makes an inversion slow and arguable is about supplying that level.

  3. You move the sparse-spike setting across its whole range. How does the agreement with the log behave?

    It peaks in the middle. Too few spikes throws away real layering, so the answer goes blocky and wrong. Too many starts fitting noise as though it were geology. The spike count is an assumption about the earth rather than a quality dial, and nothing in the seismic tells you the right value. You would find it by trying values at a well. Note also that a tidy, blocky result is not evidence of a good one.

  4. A contractor offers stochastic inversion. What does it give you that a single answer does not?

    A range you can count. Every realization fits the data as well as the deterministic answer, and none is more likely to be right. What you gain is the ability to say that eight realizations out of twelve put the reservoir below some threshold, which is the kind of statement a single answer cannot make.

  5. Your team proposes full-waveform inversion to get better reservoir impedance. What is the concern?

    It answers a different question. FWI is aimed at a detailed velocity model, which mainly improves the image. It is not a drop-in replacement for the reservoir methods above, and it needs long offsets, low frequencies and a good starting model that many surveys do not have. It also takes far more computing than the reservoir methods, though the mismatch of purpose is the more important point.

  6. You have one well and a post-stack volume, and you want a first look at where the soft rock is. What would you start with?

    Relative impedance. It needs no background model and answers exactly the question asked, which is where the soft rock is rather than what its impedance value is. Pre-stack inversion needs angle data you do not have. Stochastic inversion needs a statistical description of the rock that one well will not support. Begin with the method that needs least, and add inputs only when a question arrives that it cannot settle.

MHow this page works

The arithmetic, and what it leaves out

Everything on this page is computed while you watch, from the Maui-4 impedance log. All four methods run on the same trace with the same wavelet, so the comparison is between methods and not between setups.

Scoring. The three relative methods are scored against the log filtered to the band the wavelet actually carries, using a zero-phase filter. That is the fair test, because none of them can hold the frequencies the seismic never recorded. The model-based result is also scored against the full log, where its extra input shows up as an absolute level the others lack.

The four implementations are deliberately plain. Relative is a running sum. Colored is the summed trace passed through one filter shaped toward the log's own spectrum. Model-based is the summed trace added to a smoothed version of the log, as in module 6, rather than the iterative loop a contractor runs.

The sparse-spike panel is the loosest of the four. It keeps the largest samples of the trace, separated by at least a few samples, and sums those. A real sparse-spike inversion does something the page does not: it deconvolves the wavelet out first and looks for a small number of reflection coefficients that, once the wavelet is put back on them, reproduce the recording. That deconvolution is the method. What survives here is the assumption behind it, that the earth can be described by a few strong boundaries, and the effect of choosing how many. A trace sample is not a reflection coefficient, and module 1 is where the difference between the two is built.

Model-based and relative score the same on shape, exactly. That is not a coincidence and not a bug in the readout. The model-based result on this page is the relative result plus a background, so subtracting the background to score its shape gives the relative result back. It is the arithmetic form of what the panels show: a background model moves a curve along the impedance axis and does nothing to its shape.

The stochastic realizations are made by multiplying the answer by a slowly varying factor near one, built from random numbers with everything inside the recorded band removed. Multiplying rather than adding matters, and module 4 is where that comes from. Adding to every impedance changes the reflection coefficients through their denominators; multiplying leaves them alone. So each realization moves the values a long way while barely touching the synthetic.

How the fit is reported. Not as a raw misfit. The deterministic answer is itself band-limited, so its own synthetic already differs from the recording by around 29 percent while the noise floor is about 4 percent. Quoting the realizations' raw misfit would just repeat that baseline. The readout gives how much worse the worst realization fits than the single answer does, which comes out at a couple of percentage points.

Where this page simplifies.

  • One trace, one well. The differences between methods are clearer on a section, and some of them, colored inversion in particular, exist mainly because they are cheap over a whole volume.
  • The wavelet is known exactly. Every method here would suffer from an error in it, and they would not suffer equally.
  • Pre-stack, FWI and machine learning are named but not run. Each needs data this page does not have. The companion set demonstrates pre-stack inversion on a real well.
  • The scores depend on the scoring choice. Scored against the full log instead of the recorded band, every relative method looks much worse and the comparison stops being about the methods.

Instructor notes
Learning objectives
  • State the two questions that sort a menu: what data goes in, and how the algorithm works.
  • Name the four kinds those two questions produce, and place a method in one of them.
  • Say what pre-stack inversion returns that post-stack cannot, and why.
  • Explain what a stochastic inversion provides that a deterministic one cannot.
  • Distinguish a relative from an absolute result, and say which of the named methods returns which.
  • Say what full-waveform inversion is actually for.
Summary

Two questions sort the whole menu, and the names on it are recipes within one cell of that grid rather than alternatives to it. What separates the methods is the assumptions they make and the inputs they need, not how accurate they are. Adding a background model puts the curve on an impedance axis; it does not change the shape of the curve.

Suggested discussion questions
  • A vendor quotes for model-based inversion on a survey with no wells. What have they actually offered to do?
  • When would a blocky sparse-spike result be exactly what you want, rather than a loss?
  • Machine-learning inversion trained on three wells in the crest of a structure is applied to the flank. What could go wrong, and would you see it in the result?
Common misconceptions this page addresses
  • That the expensive method gives a better-shaped answer.
  • That stochastic inversion is more accurate rather than more honest.
  • That FWI is a better version of reservoir inversion.
Approximate completion time

Twenty minutes. Step 5's tables work well as a handout on their own, and step 4's spike control is the demonstration worth doing together. Step 1 is the one to open with on a projector: set the two controls to each combination in turn and read the cell out.
