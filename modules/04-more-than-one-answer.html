Module 4  ·  More than one answer fits

Two different rocks, one identical trace

This is the module the whole set is built around. Modules 1 to 3 showed what a survey records. This one shows what it fails to record, by having you build two different arrangements of rock that a survey could not tell apart.

Model B, against model A

The survey

Three short steps. Step 1 is the one to be surprised by.

1Two different rocks, the same trace to the last decimal

Move the multiplier anywhere you like

Model A is a section of rock: some shale, a soft sand, more shale, and a hard bed at the base. Model B is the same section with every impedance multiplied by the number on the control.

At a multiplier of 1.6, every layer in B is 60 percent harder than the layer it matches in A. A geologist looking at the two logs would describe two different sections. The right panel shows the traces those two sections produce, and they lie on top of one another. The readout gives the difference between them sample by sample, and it holds at zero wherever the multiplier is put.

The arithmetic behind that is short. A reflection coefficient is the difference between two impedances divided by their sum. Multiplying both layers by 1.6 multiplies the difference by 1.6 and the sum by 1.6, and the ratio is unchanged. Every reflection in the section comes out the same, so the trace built from them comes out the same.

This is non-uniqueness in its cleanest form. It is a property of what a seismic survey measures, not a shortcoming of this model: a recording of reflections carries the ratios between impedances and does not carry their sizes. Any inversion of that recording faces the same limit, however it is done and however good the data is.

Common misconception

"A better survey would tell these two apart."

Raise the frequency to 70 Hz and drop the noise to zero, which is a better survey than anyone will ever shoot. The traces stay identical. The information that separates A from B was never in a reflection to begin with, so no recording of reflections can contain it, and no processing can put it back. Modules 5 and 6 are about where it has to come from instead: a well, and what it takes to use one.

Try the other control

The second control adds the same amount to every impedance instead of multiplying. That one does change the trace a little, because adding a constant changes the sums in the denominators without changing the differences. Move it and watch a small difference appear. The multiplier is the exact case; the addition is close but not exact.

2The bright spot that could be anything

Move the thickness and watch the brightness stay put

Step 1 changed a whole section at once. This step changes a single bed, which is the form of non-uniqueness most likely to turn up in a project.

A bright spot is usually mapped by reading the size of the peak at each trace, and that single number is what goes onto the map. The left panel shows a sand bed at the thickness set on the control, and beside it the bed that would be needed at a different thickness to produce the same peak.

Moving the thickness from 4 m to 34 m finds a bed at every thickness that makes the same bright spot: a thin bed with a large contrast, or a thick bed with a small one. Those are different rocks and different volumes, and the amplitude map holds one number that fits all of them.

Contrast and thickness are not the only pair. A soft organic shale, a gas sand and a high-porosity brine sand can all sit at the impedance the bright bed needs, so even a thickness settled by other means leaves the lithology and the fluid open. Separating those takes angle information, well control, or a rock physics model — none of which is in the amplitude.

Why this matters

An amplitude map turns each trace into one number, and one number cannot carry two quantities. Thickness and contrast go in and a single brightness comes out, so the map cannot say which combination produced it. This is why volume estimates made from amplitude alone carry the range they do.

3What narrows it down

Turn each one on and watch the set of answers shrink

Non-uniqueness means the data alone does not choose between the models that fit, so something else has to. That something is called prior information, and each box below adds a piece of it. With all three clear, 31 different beds fit the bright spot. Adding them one at a time changes the count.

The counts quoted below are the ones the readouts give at the settings this page opens on. They move with the wavelet frequency, because a different wavelet makes a different family of beds fit. Read them off the panel rather than from here if you have changed anything.

Know what the rock can be. A sandstone at this depth cannot have any impedance you like. Ruling out the impossible takes 31 beds down to 29, which is a small gain.

Tie it to a well. A well measures the impedance of the sand where it was drilled. That takes the count to 27 and cuts the thinnest bed from 4 m to 8 m, which is also modest.

Use the whole wiggle, not just its size. This one takes 31 beds down to 2. A thin bed and a thick one produce differently shaped events even when their peaks match, and that shape was on the trace all along. Reading a single number off a map discards it. Almost all of the narrowing on this page comes from that one box.

Two beds still fit at the end, and that is the honest result rather than a failure of the exercise. Prior information narrows the set of models; it does not collapse the set to one. An inversion result is a member of whatever set is left, and how large that set is depends on how much was added and how good it was.

That is the plainest argument for inversion there is. An inversion uses the whole wiggle. An amplitude map uses one number from it.

Common misconception

"Once the inversion has run, the ambiguity is gone."

An inversion returns one model because it was told to, not because only one fits. Two of the three boxes here are decisions somebody made rather than things the seismic measured. What the rock is allowed to be, and what the well says. The result you are shown is one member of a set, and the honest question about any inversion is how wide that set was and which assumptions narrowed it. On a real section, with an estimated wavelet and beds interfering with each other, the set is much wider than this page's two.

WWhy this matters

The idea the whole set is built around

The forward direction has one answer. The backward direction has many. That is not a shortcoming of any particular method, and a better algorithm does not fix it.

Step 1 is the sharpest form of it. Scale every impedance in a section and the reflections do not change at all, so no recording of reflections can tell you which scaling is right. The absolute level of an impedance volume is not something the seismic knows.

Step 2 is the everyday form. A number read off an amplitude map is one number carrying two quantities, and it cannot be taken apart without help.

Step 3 is what practitioners actually do. They add information. Wells, geology, rock physics, an assumption about how simple the answer should be. Each addition narrows the set. None of them comes from the seismic, and each is a decision that can be right or wrong.

So the useful question about an impedance volume is not whether it is correct. It is which assumptions narrowed it, and whether you agree with them. Module 7 turns that into a short list you can ask out loud.

CCheck yourself

Four questions. Wrong answers are the useful ones

  1. You multiply every impedance in a section by 1.6. What happens to the seismic trace?

    Nothing at all. Every reflection coefficient is a difference divided by a sum, and multiplying everything scales the difference and the sum by the same amount. The traces are identical to the last decimal place, which step 1 lets you check at any multiplier you like.

  2. Given that, what can a seismic survey never tell you on its own?

    The absolute level. Boundaries and their polarity are exactly what reflections carry, and the trace reports both. What no reflection carries is where the whole section sits on the impedance scale. That has to come from a well, which is what modules 5 and 6 are about.

  3. A bright spot on an amplitude map. What does the brightness on its own tell you?

    A combination, and the map cannot take it apart. Step 2 finds a bed at every thickness from 4 m to 34 m that produces exactly the same peak, by trading contrast against thickness. A thin bed with a strong contrast and a thick one with a weak contrast are the same number on the map.

  4. An inversion returns a single impedance volume. Why only one, when many models fit?

    Assumptions chose one. Each of these picks among models the data was equally happy with. A background model from wells, a limit on how far the answer may move, an assumption about how few layers there should be. The volume is one member of a set, and the assumptions that selected it are usually not printed on the map.

If question 1 surprised you

Go to step 1 and drag the multiplier from 0.4 to 2.5 with the trace-difference readout in view. It reads zero the whole way, at every frequency and with the noise off. That one fact is why inversion needs information from outside the seismic.

MHow this page works

The arithmetic, and what it leaves out

Everything on this page is computed while you watch. The functions are in assets/physics.js.

Step 1 is exact, not approximate. Multiplying every impedance by a constant leaves every reflection coefficient algebraically unchanged, so the two traces agree to the limit of the computer's arithmetic. The readout reports the measured difference rather than assuming it, and it stays at zero.

The second control adds a constant instead of multiplying, and that one does change the trace, because adding to both impedances changes the sum in the denominator without changing the difference in the numerator. It is included so the exact case can be told from the nearly-exact one.

Step 2 searches for real. For each thickness, the page tries impedances across the whole plausible range and keeps the one whose peak comes closest to the reference bed's peak. Nothing is fitted in advance.

Step 3 measures each constraint rather than assuming it helps. The result is not what the usual account suggests. At the default 30 Hz, rock bounds take 31 beds to 29. A well tie takes it to 27. Using the whole wiggle takes it to 2. The three counts change with the frequency; the ordering does not. The ambiguity in step 2 belongs to an amplitude read off a map, not to the trace. Given the complete wiggle, a known wavelet and known surrounding rock, thickness and contrast separate well.

That is the honest shape of this page's finding, and it has a limit. The waveform test here is generous. Perfect wavelet, no interference from neighbouring beds, and only two unknowns. Every one of those is false on a real section, and each makes the surviving set larger. The scaling ambiguity in step 1, by contrast, is exact and no amount of waveform helps with it at all.

Where this page simplifies.

  • The wavelet is known exactly and is symmetrical. In practice it is estimated, and an error in it widens every range on this page.
  • The rock above and below the bed is known. In practice it is not, which adds more unknowns and more ambiguity than step 3 shows.
  • Only two things vary in step 2. A real section has many layers whose reflections overlap, so the true set of models that fit is far larger than a two-parameter search can show.
  • The whole-waveform test here is generous. It assumes a perfect wavelet, no interference from neighbouring beds and controllable noise, so the narrowing it achieves is a best case.
  • No angles. Recording the same reflection at several angles adds information and narrows the set further. That belongs to the advanced set.
Instructor notes
Learning objectives
  • Show that scaling every impedance leaves the trace unchanged.
  • State what a seismic survey therefore cannot determine on its own.
  • Explain why a single amplitude cannot separate thickness from contrast.
  • Name three kinds of information that narrow the set, and say which does the most.
Summary

The inverse problem has many answers. An inversion returns one because assumptions chose it, not because the data did.

Suggested discussion questions
  • Two contractors invert the same volume and hand back different impedance levels. Both fit the seismic. What would you ask each of them?
  • Your prospect is mapped on amplitude alone. What range of volumes is consistent with the map, and what one piece of data would most reduce it?
  • Step 3 shows the whole wiggle doing far more than the well tie or the rock bounds. Why might an interpreter still work from an amplitude map?
Common misconceptions this page addresses
  • That a better survey would resolve the ambiguity in step 1.
  • That running an inversion removes the ambiguity rather than choosing within it.
Approximate completion time

Twenty minutes. Step 1 is worth doing on a projector: dragging the multiplier while the difference readout sits at zero makes the point in about ten seconds.
