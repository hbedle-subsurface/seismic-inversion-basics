Somebody hands you an impedance map. How much of it do you believe?

Most of every number on that map came from a well, not from the seismic. Several different arrangements of rock would have produced the same recording. Somebody chose which one you are looking at, and the assumptions behind that choice are almost never printed on the map.

None of that makes the map wrong. It makes it a piece of evidence with a provenance, and reading it well means knowing which parts came from the ground and which came from a decision. That is a skill you will use in a job interview, in a prospect review, and the first time a contractor shows you a result you are expected to have an opinion about.

Eight short modules. You change the rock and watch the seismic change, then the rock is hidden and you try to get it back. Everything is calculated in your browser while you watch.

Two things the whole set comes back to

More than one rock makes the same recording

A reflection is a difference between two rocks divided by their sum. Multiply every impedance in a section by the same number and every reflection stays where it was, so no recording and no processing of it can tell the two sections apart. The same happens on a single bed: a thin bed with a large contrast and a thick one with a small contrast make the same bright event, and a gas sand, a soft organic shale and a poorly cemented brine sand can all sit at one impedance value.

This is non-uniqueness. The information that would separate those cases was never recorded. An inversion result is one member of a set of models that all fit, and something other than the seismic chose it. Modules 1 and 4.

The lowest frequencies were never recorded

Impedance climbs steadily with depth as rocks compact, and that climb is too slow for a survey to record. A usable band from 8 Hz to 60 Hz holds nothing at all below 8 Hz, including zero. Summing a trace recovers the shape within the band and nothing outside it, so the curve comes back with the right wiggles standing on no level at all. That is relative acoustic impedance.

Turning it into numbers means supplying the missing part from wells, interpolated along horizons to make a low-frequency model. On the Maui-4 log that supplied part is about 86 percent of every value in the answer. The pattern comes from the seismic; most of the number on the color bar came from a well. Modules 5 and 6.

What this set leaves out

Four things an inversion project involves are named in module 8 but demonstrated in the companion set rather than here, because they are hard to use well before the basics are secure. They are listed so that a name met on a menu is at least familiar.

  • Wavelet estimation. Every module here is handed the wavelet. In practice it is estimated from the seismic and the wells, its phase cannot be checked from the seismic at all, and an error in it moves every answer.
  • The model-based loop. A contractor runs an iterative loop with a starting model, a misfit, a constraint and a stopping rule, each of which is a choice.
  • Prestack and elastic inversion. Keeping the angles the stack throws away gives a second rock property, which separates fluids from lithology in a way one property cannot.
  • Building a background model across a survey. Interpolating well logs along horizons is a piece of interpretation in its own right, and module 6 shows how much of the answer rests on it.

What this is, and what it is not

These are teaching models, built to make ideas visible. The arithmetic is what the literature describes, on one trace at a time and in its plainest form, so each step can be watched rather than taken on trust. Every module lists what it left out. This is not inversion software: the numbers describe the model on your screen, not your survey.

The companion set, How Seismic Inversion Actually Works, covers background models, the model-based loop, wavelet estimation, prestack inversion and reading a section, on real well data. Start here first.

About

Built for teaching by Dr. Heather Bedle and Dr. April Moreno-Ward, School of Geosciences, University of Oklahoma, with the Attribute Assisted Seismic Processing and Interpretation (AASPI) consortium.

By the end you should be able to say what acoustic impedance is, explain the difference between the forward and the inverse problem, give a reason why an inversion does not have a single answer, say what a wavelet does to a thin bed, and ask sensible questions about a result somebody else produced.

Modules 1 to 4 use simple layered models, because a made-up model can be changed while you watch and a real well cannot. Modules 5 to 8 use Maui-4, an open-file well from the Taranaki Basin, so the last thing you see is real rock.

Everything runs in the browser — no installation, no account, and nothing you do inside a module is transmitted anywhere. The one thing recorded is that a page was opened, with no cookie and nothing about you, so that the modules people use are the ones that get improved.
